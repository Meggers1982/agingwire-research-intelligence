## Bigger picture: feature pitch

**The pattern:** The September employment prints break the direction the older-worker series had been running in, and the care-sector series went the other way in the same month. BLS puts employment of people 65 years and over at 11,654 thousand in September 2026, down 0.7% year over year, after the August print of 11,753 thousand was up 2.1%. Over the same month home health care services employment reached 1,894.20 thousand jobs (+4.4%), nursing care facilities 1,587.40 thousand (+1.9%), and continuing care retirement communities and assisted living 1,022.10 thousand (+0.8%). KFF counts nearly 379,000 health sector jobs added over the past year, a 2% increase against 0.2% growth in other non-farm employment. The workforce cluster is the one beat this run where the sources actually land on the same subject rather than sharing a tag, and what they say together is that the fastest-hiring part of the economy is the part that cares for older adults, while the number of older adults holding a job is falling.

**Why it matters:** 11,654,000 people over 65 were working in September, roughly 99,000 fewer than the August print and down on the year, and that is the group for whom a job is the difference between drawing down savings and not. The jobs still being added are care jobs paying $28.14 an hour in home health (+2.4% year over year) and $25.89 in assisted living and CCRCs (+4.8%). An older worker who loses a position and looks at what is hiring is looking at direct care, at those wages, often for a neighbor their own age.

**Why pitch this now:** The September figures printed this month and will be overwritten by the October print; four of these series were first collected since the last pitch ran on 09/28/26, and the 65-and-over number moved from +2.1% to -0.7% between prints. Federal fiscal 2027 began 10/01/26, so operator budgets for the hiring year just took effect against these wage levels.

**Angle:** Whether the only labor market still expanding for older adults is the one that takes care of them, and what happens to the 65-and-over worker who was counted in August and not in September. Working title: "The Care Economy Is Hiring. Older Workers Are Not." For readers in their sixties and seventies who planned to work longer, and for the operators bidding for the same hours.

## The pitch

In September, BLS counted 11,654 thousand people aged 65 and over with a job, down 0.7% from a year earlier, after August's print of 11,753 thousand was up 2.1%. In the same month, the industries that care for that age group kept hiring: home health care services at 1,894.20 thousand jobs, up 4.4%; nursing care facilities up 1.9%; assisted living and continuing care communities up 0.8%. KFF counts nearly 379,000 health sector jobs added over the year, 2% growth against 0.2% everywhere else. I would like to write about 1,400 words on what that split means for someone who planned to work past 65: which jobs are actually being added, what they pay ($28.14 an hour in home health, $25.89 in assisted living and CCRCs), and why the openings an older job seeker finds are increasingly care work. It rests entirely on federal series readers can look up themselves, with a chart of the four lines over the past year. Next Avenue's readers sit on both sides of this at once.
