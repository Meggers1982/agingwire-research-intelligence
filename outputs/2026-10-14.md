## Bigger picture: feature pitch

**The pattern:** Three KFF briefs published on 10/13/26 describe, between them, a door that only opens once. "Nearly Three-Quarters of Medicare Advantage Enrollees Could be Denied a Medigap Policy Due to a Pre-Existing Condition" finds that 74% of Medicare Advantage enrollees, or 20.9 million people in 2023, have a condition that could trigger a denial if they try to move to traditional Medicare outside their one-time 12-month trial period. "Medigap Guaranteed Issue Requirements: An Overview of Federal and State Rules" sets out the federal floor and the state rules layered on top of it, including what happens to beneficiaries under 65 with long-term disabilities. "Key Facts About Medigap Enrollment and Premiums for Medicare Beneficiaries" supplies the enrollment and premium variation by state. Together they make it possible to say, state by state, whether a person's ability to leave Medicare Advantage depends on their health or on their address.

**Why it matters:** 20.9 million people made a coverage choice whose reversal is now conditional on medical underwriting in most of the country. Someone who picks Medicare Advantage at 65, develops a condition at 72 and then wants traditional Medicare plus a supplement may find that no insurer has to sell them one, which leaves them in a plan with a network they can no longer use or in traditional Medicare with no cap on cost sharing. The guaranteed-issue brief is the piece that tells a reader which of those outcomes their own state law produces, and that is the one of the three no monitored publisher has matched.

**Why pitch this now:** The three briefs landed on 10/13/26, within a day of each other, and the September 2026 medical care consumer price index printed at 593.65 on the 1982-84 base, up 1.5% year over year, so the out-of-pocket exposure that a supplement covers is still climbing. The 12-month trial period is a clock that starts the day someone enrolls, which makes this a story with a per-reader deadline rather than a policy cycle.

**Angle:** Whether the right to buy a Medigap policy is a federal right or a local accident, and what the 20.9 million people in the KFF estimate can actually do about it before their trial period closes. Working title: "The Twelve Months Nobody Told You Were a Deadline." For older adults, their adult children, and the people advising them on coverage.

**Potential headlines:**
- Switching back to traditional Medicare can require passing a health review
- Your state decides whether a Medigap insurer can turn you down
- Twenty-one million Medicare Advantage enrollees have a condition that could cost them a supplement

**Potential outlets:**
- **Bankrate Retirement** — readers run Medicare comparisons with a calculator open, and guaranteed issue is the variable missing from every premium table they are using.
- **MarketWatch Retirement** — treats retirement decisions as irreversible financial commitments, which is exactly what the one-time trial period makes of a plan choice at 65.
- **Next Avenue** — the state-rules variation is a human story about two people with the same diagnosis and different zip codes, which is this outlet's register.
- **Caring.com** — its audience is at the decision point, often choosing coverage in the same month they are choosing care, and the under-65 disability rules matter to a slice of them directly.

## The pitch

On 10/13/26 KFF put out three briefs on Medigap that have not been read together. One finds that 74% of Medicare Advantage enrollees, 20.9 million people in 2023, have a pre-existing condition that could get a Medigap application denied if they try to switch to traditional Medicare after their one-time 12-month trial period ends. A second lays out the federal guaranteed issue rules and the state rules stacked on top, including the treatment of people under 65 with long-term disabilities. A third gives enrollment and premium variation by state. I would write 1,200 words that answer one question your readers ask every fall: can I still change my mind, and what does my state let an insurer do about it. Medical care prices were up 1.5% year over year in September 2026, so the cost of guessing wrong is rising. Bankrate's Medicare coverage already does the math; this adds the rule that decides whether the math is available.

**Most Medicare Advantage enrollees could be turned down for a supplement**
*Nearly Three-Quarters of Medicare Advantage Enrollees Could be Denied a Medigap Policy Due to a Pre-Existing Condition*  
- Angle: Run the 74% and 20.9 million figures against the one-time 12-month trial period and show what a person with a new diagnosis can and cannot do in year two.
- Hook: KFF put a number on a risk most enrollees are told nothing about when they sign up.
- For readers: Find out when your own 12-month trial period started, because after it closes a Medigap insurer in most states can review your health before deciding whether to sell you a policy.
- For the trade: Plans and brokers face a retention question that looks like loyalty in the data but is partly the absence of an exit, which should change how enrollment stability is read.
- Why it matters: A person who misses the trial-period window can be locked into a network they can no longer use, with no supplement available to soften traditional Medicare's cost sharing.
- Also: KFF gives estimates by age and by state, so this cuts to a single-state version anywhere.
