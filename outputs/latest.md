## Bigger picture: feature pitch

**The pattern:** The second year of the rural health fund is paying out at wildly different rates depending on which side of a state line a person lives on, and the files that describe the hospitals those people actually use were all refreshed in the same three weeks. KFF's analysis, posted 11/13/26, puts awards per rural resident below $100 in twelve states and above $500 in eight. CMS reissued Hospital General Information on 10/26/26, Medicare Spending Per Beneficiary - Hospital, Healthcare Associated Infections and the HCAHPS patient survey on 10/19/26, and Unplanned Hospital Visits on 10/20/26, each carrying state and county geography. Set side by side, they let a state's per-resident award be read against the named hospitals in that state and the Medicare record those hospitals already carry: episode spending from three days before admission through thirty days after discharge, infection rates, readmissions, and what discharged patients said.

**Why it matters:** A rural resident in one of the twelve states under $100 per head is usually the person with the fewest alternatives if the one hospital within reach thins its services. Nothing in the award figures tells that person anything about their own hospital. The CMS files do, down to the county, and they are the only public record in this run that answers the question a rural family actually asks: is the place I would be taken to any good, and what happens in the thirty days after they send me home. The five-to-one spread is the national story; the hospital-level files are what makes it usable.

**Why pitch this now:** The second-year award analysis published 11/13/26, the day of this run, and it is the first look at how year two differs from year one. The CMS hospital files refreshed between 10/19/26 and 10/26/26 and the survey-based ones replace the oldest quarter with the most recently completed one, so the current numbers are live and will be overwritten at the next quarterly update.

**Angle:** Whether the second year of rural health money is reaching the states whose hospitals carry the heaviest Medicare episode spending and readmission records, and what a rural family can learn about its own hospital from the October files while the question is argued at the state level. Working title: "The Hundred-Dollar States." For rural older adults and their families, and for the post-acute operators whose referrals come from those hospitals.

## The pitch

Rural health fund awards in their second year range from under $100 per rural resident in twelve states to more than $500 in eight, according to the analysis KFF posted on 11/13/26. I would like to write the version a reader in one of the low-award states can actually use. CMS refreshed its hospital files in late October: Hospital General Information on 10/26/26, Medicare Spending Per Beneficiary, Healthcare Associated Infections and the HCAHPS patient survey on 10/19/26, Unplanned Hospital Visits on 10/20/26. All carry state and county identifiers. Setting the award figures beside the named hospitals in each state gives a piece that reads nationally and breaks apart cleanly for local newsrooms: about 1,200 words plus a sortable state table. USA Today reaches the rural readers in those twelve states, and they are the ones living furthest from a second hospital if the first one narrows what it offers.

## Story ideas

**Look up your nearest hospital's overall rating before you need it**
*CMS refreshed dataset: Hospital General Information*  
- Angle: A county-level walkthrough of the Medicare-registered hospital list, with hospital type and overall rating, for older adults who have one realistic hospital within driving distance.
- Hook: The file that names every hospital registered with Medicare, with addresses, type and overall rating, was reissued on 10/26/26 and breaks to states and counties.
- For readers: Pull the entry for the hospital you would be taken to, note its type and overall rating, and check whether the next-nearest one is rated differently before an emergency makes the choice for you.
- For the trade: For post-acute and senior living operators, the type-and-rating field is the cleanest public map of which referral sources in a market are rated above or below their neighbors.
- Why it matters: If you only learn your hospital's rating after admission, you have lost the one moment when the information could have changed where you went.
- Also: Localizable to US states and counties; supports a sortable table and metro-level cuts.
- Pitch to: SeniorLiving.org, Fierce Healthcare

**What Medicare spends on the thirty days after your hospital stay, hospital by hospital**
*CMS refreshed dataset: Medicare Spending Per Beneficiary - Hospital*  
- Angle: A piece comparing hospitals on the MSPB episode, which covers Part A and Part B payments from three days before admission through 30 days after discharge, for families planning what happens after a hospital stay.
- Hook: The measure is explicitly an episode, not a stay, so a hospital's number reflects the care chain it discharges people into.
- For readers: Ask the discharge planner what happens in the 30 days after you leave, and compare the hospital's spending-per-episode standing against hospitals nationally before you agree to a post-acute placement.
- For the trade: Hospitals carrying high episode spending have the strongest incentive to tighten their post-acute networks, which decides which skilled nursing and home health providers keep referrals.
- Why it matters: The thirty days after discharge is where readmissions and unplanned costs land, and this is the only public figure that puts a hospital's whole episode in one number.
- Also: State and county geography; pairs naturally with the readmissions file for a two-measure chart.
- Pitch to: Modern Healthcare, Bankrate Retirement

**How often patients end up back in the hospital after they are sent home**
*CMS refreshed dataset: Unplanned Hospital Visits - Hospital*  
- Angle: A readmissions and return-days explainer built from the provider-level file, for families weighing a discharge date they feel is too early.
- Hook: The file carries excess days in acute care, unplanned readmissions and unplanned visits after outpatient procedures in one provider-level record, refreshed 10/20/26.
- For readers: Before you accept a discharge plan, look up the hospital's unplanned return record and ask what support is arranged for the first week at home.
- For the trade: Operators taking referrals from a hospital with heavy return days are taking on the clinical risk that shows up in that hospital's numbers.
- Why it matters: A second admission within weeks is the most common way a short stay turns into a long-term care decision, and the pattern is on the public record before it happens to you.
- Also: Provider level with state and county rollups; strong candidate for a metro ranking.
- Pitch to: McKnight's Long-Term Care News, Skilled Nursing News

**The infections patients pick up in the hospital are on the public record**
*CMS refreshed dataset: Healthcare Associated Infections - Hospital*  
- Angle: A hospital-by-hospital read of the CDC-developed infection measures collected through the National Healthcare Safety Network, for older patients facing a planned admission with a central line or catheter.
- Hook: The measures cover device-related infections that occur while a patient is in the hospital and are reported at provider level, refreshed 10/19/26.
- For readers: If a procedure is scheduled, ask which devices will be used and check the hospital's infection measures for those device types first.
- For the trade: Infection performance at referring hospitals shapes what post-acute providers inherit on admission and what they will be held to afterward.
- Why it matters: Device-related infection is one of the few hospital risks a patient can weigh in advance, and the measure exists specifically so they can.
- Also: CDC-developed measures via NHSN; county and state cuts available.;
- Pitch to: Skilled Nursing News, Family Caregiver Alliance

**What other patients said about the hospital you are being sent to**
*CMS refreshed dataset: Patient survey (HCAHPS) - Hospital*  
- Angle: A read of the national standardized inpatient survey at hospital level, for families choosing between two hospitals in the same market.
- Hook: HCAHPS is a national, standardized survey of recent inpatients, which makes hospital-to-hospital comparison fair in a way that online reviews are not.
- For readers: Compare the survey results for the two hospitals nearest you, and weigh them against the travel time you would have to accept to reach the better-rated one.
- For the trade: Survey standing is a marketing asset and a referral filter, and it moves with staffing levels operators are already struggling to hold.
- Why it matters: Patient experience is the part of hospital quality a family can verify independently, and the survey is the only version of it collected the same way everywhere.
- Also: Refreshed 10/19/26; state and county geography supports local comparison tables.
- Pitch to: McKnight's Long-Term Care News, Bankrate Retirement

- Why it matters: A discharge without a transition record or continued medication is the most preventable way a psychiatric admission turns into the next one.
- Also: State level with matching facility and national files for a three-tier chart.
- Pitch to: Fierce Healthcare, SeniorLiving.org
